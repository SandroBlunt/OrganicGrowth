## Angle
Every other AI money story today is abstract. Roughly $500 billion of infrastructure financing being
assembled, a manifesto pledging $1 billion to host communities, none of it something a viewer can see
from a window. This one is different: OpenAI reportedly completed a $7 billion tender offer, meaning
employees turned paper shares into spendable money, and TechCrunch ties that to renewed trouble in San
Francisco's housing market. The tension: the AI boom is usually described in data-center units, and here
it shows up in rent. It is the same boom, measured in the one unit everyone understands.

## Hook concept
Open on the translation: $7 billion of stock became actual money in actual bank accounts, in one city,
in one summer. The surprise is that the most visible effect of the AI boom on ordinary life so far is not
a robot or a chatbot, it is the price of a two-bedroom. (Concept only, writer lands the final line.)

## Suggested Recipe
Both, per the Format. **News Carousel (7 slides)** because the arc is a translation chain: what a tender

is the company organising a garage sale for its own staff's shares, so nobody has to wait for an IPO.

## Talking points
- OpenAI reportedly completed a $7 billion tender offer that let employees cash out shares (TechCrunch,
  Aug 10). Reportedly is the operative word, one outlet has it.
- What a tender offer is, plainly: the company arranges a sale so existing shareholders, usually staff,
  can sell to incoming investors without the company going public.
- TechCrunch connects the payout to renewed trouble in San Francisco's housing market, which is the
  second-order effect most AI coverage never reaches.
- Scale comparison inside the same day, using two reported figures side by side: about $7 billion to
  employees, against roughly $500 billion of AI infrastructure financing being assembled by Apollo,
  Blackstone, Goldman Sachs and others with Nvidia (FT, NYT). The people cost and the machine cost are
  not in the same league.
- The important distinction for anyone reading the number as a health signal: a tender is existing
  shareholders selling to new ones, so it puts no new money into OpenAI and says nothing about revenue.
- The you-first read: this is where AI wealth actually touches a normal person's life, through the cost
  of living in the few zip codes where it concentrates.
- The fair counterpoint: employee liquidity at a large private company is ordinary and expected, not a
  scandal. The story is the size and the concentration, not the existence of the sale.

## The catch (mandatory skeptic beat)
One outlet, one word doing heavy lifting: reportedly. There is no disclosed count of how many employees
sold, at what valuation, or how much of it went anywhere near property, and the housing connection is a
link drawn in a news story rather than a measured finding. Housing markets move on interest rates,
supply and policy at the same time, so treat "AI money is raising your rent" as an unproven claim and
tell the viewer that plainly. The verified part is the $7 billion tender itself, and even that is
reported, not confirmed by the company in this evidence.
