## Angle
Read the essay as what it actually is: a governance document written by a company, about powers no
company has been given. "The Future is for Everyone" runs past 6,000 words, says "superintelligent"
about 60 times, and takes positions on regulation, jobs, cybersecurity, bioweapon risk and surveillance,
with one number attached, a $1 billion pledge to communities that host Meta's data centers. The tension:
the most detailed public plan for how superintelligence should be handled was published by the party
with the most to gain from the answer, and on the same day a US senator's counter-position amounted to
"please stop". Two documents pointing opposite ways, neither with a mechanism in it.

## Hook concept
Open on the word count, not the ideas: one company, 6,000 words, roughly 60 uses of "superintelligent",
zero binding commitments. The surprise is that the closest thing to an AI policy anyone published this
week came from a company, not a government, and the only firm number in it is a cheque to the neighbours.
(Concept only, writer lands the final line.)

## Suggested Recipe
Both, per the Format. **News Carousel (7 slides)** works as a document autopsy: the word-frequency card,

which is a thing nobody has built and everybody is now planning around.

## Talking points
- The essay is titled "The Future is for Everyone", runs past 6,000 words (Fast Company counts ~6,500)
  and uses "superintelligent" roughly 60 times, per The Guardian's lead story of Aug 10.
- Its scope is not product: it covers data centers, regulation, cybersecurity, bioweapon risk, labor
  disruption and surveillance. That is a policy agenda, published by a company.
- The one hard commitment in it is money: $1 billion pledged to communities that host Meta's data
  centers (Fast Company's six-things summary).
- FT reads the same document as competitive positioning: "Mark Zuckerberg attacks 'closed' AI rivals as
  Meta returns to open models". The philosophy and the business strategy are the same sentence.
- TechCrunch's verdict is blunt: the manifesto "is exactly why people don't like AI". That is a
  mainstream tech outlet, not a critic on the fringe.
- The Verge ran two pieces on it the same day, including "Four takeaways from Mark Zuckerberg's massive
  AI manifesto", and framed the vision as dim rather than inspiring.
- NYT DealBook asked the business question under the philosophy: why Meta is betting big on open AI,
  which is the part that actually predicts Meta's next two years.
- Same-day contrast worth naming once: Senator Bernie Sanders asked AI companies to pause development
  (Guardian, NYT). One document plans the destination, the other asks for a stop, and neither has any
  enforcement attached.

## The catch (mandatory skeptic beat)
An essay is not a policy. There are no dates in it, no external audit, no third party who gets to check
anything, and nothing a reader could hold Meta to next year. Repeating "superintelligent" 60 times also
does quiet work: it makes today's models feel like step one of something inevitable, when the actual
thing Meta shipped that day was a 30B model that runs on a gaming card. Treat the word count as
positioning, and judge the company on the releases and the data-center permits instead.
