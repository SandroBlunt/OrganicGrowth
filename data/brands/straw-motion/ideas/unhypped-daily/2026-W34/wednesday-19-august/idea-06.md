# Someone hid an AirTag in donated rare books, and 404 Media followed it to an Amazon AI facility

- **Corroboration: FLAGGED, effectively single source.** Two links, but only one is original reporting: 404 Media ran the investigation, and Ars Technica is following it. There is no Amazon response in this evidence and no second independent investigation. Attribute everything.

## Suggested Recipe
Both, per the Format. **News Carousel** rides the method: hook on the tracker, then the destination, then why books specifically are worth this much to a model builder, then the you-first turn about donation bins, then the catch that one shipment is one shipment. **News Short Script** carries the same spine and makes the attribution caution part of the script, not a footnote.

## Angle
The tension is between what donating books is supposed to mean and where a tracked box actually went. 404 Media hid an AirTag in a shipment of donated rare books and followed it, and its own headline says the shipment ended at an Amazon AI training facility. The contrast to ride: a second life on somebody's shelf, versus a scanner. And the reason this lands harder than any other training-data story is that it is not an argument, it is a location history.

## Hook Concept
The surprise is the method, so open on the method rather than the conclusion. Somebody put a consumer Apple tracker in a box of donated rare books just to find out where they really go. Then the destination. The reframe: this is not an opinion about how AI gets trained, it is a map, and the tool that made it costs less than a dinner. Concept only, the writer lands the final line.

## Talking Points
- 404 Media ran the investigation itself, and its headline is the finding: "We tracked a shipment of rare books. It ended at an Amazon AI training facility." That is original reporting, not a rewrite of a press release.
- The method is the story: a hidden AirTag, Apple's own consumer tracker, used as a piece of journalism. Anyone watching this owns or can buy the same tool, which is what makes the result feel checkable rather than theoretical.
- Ars Technica's follow-up headline goes further, saying the AirTag "reveals Amazon is trashing rare books to train AI" (Aug 18). Treat "trashing" as Ars Technica's characterisation of 404 Media's finding, and say so on the card. This is a reported finding, not an established fact.
- Plain English for why books at all: models are trained on text, and scanned books are unusually good text. Long, edited, coherent, and written by people who were paid to be clear. That is why a warehouse of them is worth something to a model builder.
- The you-first turn: if you have ever dropped a box at a donation bin, the honest answer to "where did those go" is that you do not have one. That is uncomfortable, and it is now verifiable for about the price of a tracker.
- Sourcing note that belongs on screen: this rests on one original investigation and one follow-up carry, with no Amazon statement in the evidence. Say that in the post rather than hiding it.

## The Catch (mandatory skeptic beat)
One tracked shipment is one shipment. It does not establish a policy, a scale, or intent, and no count of books appears anywhere in this evidence. The story is strong because of how it was found, not because of how much it proves. Amazon has not responded here, and a single AirTag cannot tell you what happened to the books after they arrived. Report the route, attribute the interpretation, and resist the urge to turn one box into a company-wide verdict.

## To Verify At Production
Whether 404 Media published tracker screenshots or a route map, how many books were in the shipment, which facility it was, whether Amazon responded anywhere, and what 404 Media says actually happens to the books on arrival. Pull only from the two linked pieces.
